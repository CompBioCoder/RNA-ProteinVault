> [!abstract] Abstract
> Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality despite major advances in pharmacological therapy, interventional cardiology, and lifestyle-based prevention. Although many current treatments reduce disease burden and improve outcomes, they often fail to address the underlying molecular and genetic mechanisms driving disease progression dot CRISPR hyphen-based gene- editing systems (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) have introduced the possibility of directly correcting, silencing, or modulating pathogenic genetic determinants involved in cardiovascular pathology. When integrated with artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled design, prediction, and optimization tools, an AI-guided CRISPR framework may assist in prioritizing editing strategies, predicting editing outcomes, and informing delivery design, although cardiovascular-specific validation of these applications remains limited. This narrative review examines the potential role of AI-guided CRISPR technologies in cardiovascular disease. It first outlines the major pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying CVD, then reviews molecular and genetic targets that may be amenable to editing. Current CRISPR applications in cardiometabolic and cardiovascular disorders are discussed, followed by an evaluation of how AI may support guide RNA design, off-target prediction, vector engineering, and therapeutic prioritization. Finally, major translational barriers are examined, including tissue-specific delivery, editing in non-dividing cells, long-term genomic safety, disease complexity, and regulatory implementation. Although early preclinical findings are encouraging, most cardiovascular applications remain in the experimental or early translational stage, and direct evidence for integrated AI-guided CRISPR applications in cardiovascular disease remains limited and largely preclinical. Progress in this field will depend on advances in delivery science, genome engineering, computational modelling, and long-term safety validation. AI-guided CRISPR should therefore be regarded as a developing precision medicine strategy with substantial future potential rather than an immediately deployable clinical solution.
