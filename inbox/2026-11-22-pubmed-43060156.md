> [!abstract] Abstract
> hnRNPA2 is a component of cellular membraneless organelles many of which contain RNA and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). RBPs frequently contain low-complexity domains (LCDs) that can also assemble into amyloid fibrils. Fibrils isolated from patients with frontotemporal lobar degeneration contain ordered segments of these LCDs and disease-associated mutations can enhance the homotypic interactions that promote aggregation. Although previous studies have largely examined the condensate-to-fibril transition by altering condensate stability, how amyloid structure governs this process remains poorly understood. Here, we show that the hnRNPA2 LCD forms a reproducible fibril fold and by combining structure-guided thermodynamic profiling, peptide design, mutagenesis and in vitro characterization under conditions with (two-phase) and without (one-phase) condensates, we define the molecular determinants of its assembly. We identify an aromatic triad within the fibril core that controls fibril formation kinetics and thermodynamic stability. Mutational analysis further reveals that condensate stability and fibril stability are largely independent. Finally, microrheology linked reduced condensate viscosity to faster fibril formation, supporting a role for condensate material properties in regulating aggregation kinetics. Together, these findings support a model in which the condensate-to-fibril transition is governed by the interplay between a disordered "fuzzy coat" and a fibril-forming aromatic triad within hnRNPA2. This work provides a structural framework for understanding how condensates can transition into amyloid assemblies. Protein condensates may promote or suppress amyloid formation, but the properties governing these outcomes remain poorly understood. Through mutational analysis, this study examines the relationships among condensate material properties, condensate and fibril stability, and aggregation kinetics. The findings provide a framework for understanding how changes in condensate properties influence amyloid formation in the system studied, with potential implications for disease-associated protein assembly.
