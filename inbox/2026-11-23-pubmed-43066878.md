PubMed · 2026-11-21 · Mol Neurobiol · [原文](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43066878/)

> [!abstract] Abstract
> Stress granules (SGs) are dynamic, membraneless ribonucleoprotein condensates that assemble in response to oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, nutrient deprivation, and proteotoxic stress. Transient SG formation reduces the global translational burden and supports acute stress adaptation in many experimental settings. During aging and chronic disease, persistent oxidative stress, bioenergetic failure, mutations in RNA binding proteins, and impaired autophagy-lysosome function can disrupt SG resolution and composition and, in defined experimental systems, are associated with reduced molecular exchange, gel-like behavior, or solid-like aggregation. Persistent or compositionally aberrant SG states can alter the localization, interactions, and phase behavior of disease-associated proteins, thereby perturbing RNA metabolism, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and proteostasis. Importantly, SGs are not uniformly pathogenic: competent, transient SG responses can limit neurotoxicity in selected settings, whereas persistent or compositionally aberrant SGs have been shown to facilitate pathological protein aggregation in selected experimental systems. A more rational therapeutic objective is therefore not indiscriminate inhibition of SG assembly, but the identification of state-specific markers, restoration of condensate reversibility, and selective removal of aberrant granules. This review examines the SG life cycle, the transition from physiological to pathological states, the strength of evidence across major neurodegenerative diseases, and the translational challenges associated with targeting SG dynamics. Particular emphasis is placed on cell type, stress context, disease stage, and the distinction between SG-dependent and SG-independent protein aggregation.
