> [!abstract] Abstract
> Organisms enhance their fitness and survival by organizing macromolecules into distinct physical states at the subcellular level. Multivalent macromolecules undergo phase separation under conditions set by their composition and environment, allowing cellular components to be spatially segregated and functionally coordinated. Through this process, membraneless organelles, also known as biomolecular condensates, are formed, thereby facilitating dynamic compartmentalization of cellular components that is essential for diverse biological processes. These biomolecular condensates display distinct dynamic biophysical behaviors, including liquid-like and gel/solid-like states. Through these dynamic behaviors, biomolecular condensates can regulate the redistribution of RNA and RNA-binding proteins, as well as mRNA translation and stability, under stress conditions. In plants, biomolecular condensates, particularly stress granules, contribute to development and stress adaptation by shaping gene expression, RNA processing, and protein interactions; however, their material properties have rarely been measured, and whether these properties themselves contribute to stress tolerance remains to be established. In the present review, we explore the key biomolecules that drive liquid-liquid phase separation in plants under diverse environmental conditions, examine recent findings on how the dynamic biophysical behavior of biomolecular condensates relates to plant stress responses, and consider plant-specific physical features, such as the vacuole, cytoplasmic streaming, and turgor, that distinguish plant condensates from those in animal cells.
