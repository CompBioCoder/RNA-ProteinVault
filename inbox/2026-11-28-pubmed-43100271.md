> [!abstract] Abstract
> S-adenosyl-methionine (SAM)-dependent methyltransferases (MTases) are one of the largest and most ancient enzyme superfamilies in biology, transferring a methyl group from SAM to substrates as different as DNA, RNA, proteins, and small-molecule metabolites. Most SAM-MTases share a Class I Rossmann-like SAM-binding core, with substrate specificity determined by variable inserts and accessory domains rather than the conserved fold; as a result, substrate type is unknown for most MTases in sequence databases, which are dominated by unreviewed entries. Here we present SMSTPred (SAM-Methyltransferase Substrate-Type Predictor), a multi-label classifier that predicts MTase substrate type from protein language model embeddings and benchmarks it under homology-disjoint cross-validation against sequence-homology, structure-homology, and profile-HMM baselines. SMSTPred reaches a cluster-disjoint Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.94, significantly ahead of sequence-homology (MMseqs2, 0.85), structure-homology (Foldseek, 0.89) and profile-HMM (0.52) baselines, while providing calibrated, per-substrate confidence. Applying it to ∼116,000 MTases across 1,249 reference proteomes, we map the substrate landscape of methylation across the tree of life. Substrate specialization tracks taxonomy: animals expand protein- and DNA-methylation, whereas small-molecule methyltransferases are enriched in plants and Actinomycetota; in Actinomycetota beyond what is expected for their proteome size, and in plants in proportion to their larger proteomes. The predictions identify thousands of uncharacterized methyltransferases with substrate type assignments (about half of those in uncharacterized clusters), many corroborated by an independent reaction-retrieval method. Notably, ∼11% of enzymes with a single substrate call lack any class-discriminative sequence motif, suggesting that substrate specificity is often encoded beyond short linear motifs. Together, this work provides SMSTPred, a substrate-type predictor for Class I methyltransferases, and a tree-of-life resource of testable functional hypotheses.
