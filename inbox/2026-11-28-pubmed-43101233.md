PubMed · 2026-11-17 · Rev Cardiovasc Med · [原文](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43101233/)

> [!abstract] Abstract
> Cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (CIRP) is an evolutionarily conserved, stress-responsive protein that plays a critical, complex dual role in the occurrence and progression of myocardial infarction. As an intracellular stress sensor, CIRP enhances cardiac cell adaptation to ischemic stress and mitigates early injury by regulating messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) stability, antioxidant pathways, apoptosis inhibition, and energy metabolism. CIRP expression is induced by signals such as hypothermia, oxidative stress, and hypoxia, and its cytoprotective functions are activated through post-translational modifications, including arginine methylation and phosphorylation, which facilitate nucleocytoplasmic translocation. However, upon cardiac cell necrosis or pyroptosis, CIRP can be actively secreted or passively released into the extracellular space, where it acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern. CIRP then binds to receptors, such as toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells-1 (TREM-1), on the surface of macrophages and neutrophils, activating inflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), thereby amplifying sterile inflammation, exacerbating myocardial injury, and delaying repair. Furthermore, extracellular CIRP is involved in regulating myocardial fibrosis, angiogenesis, and arrhythmias, with effects that exhibit significant temporal dynamics and cell specificity. Studies have shown that therapeutic strategies targeting CIRP, including receptor antagonists, RNA-binding domain inhibitors, and neutralizing/blocking approaches, can improve cardiac function, reduce infarct size, and attenuate inflammatory responses in animal models. Despite challenges such as its functional duality and clinical translation, CIRP has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic target for myocardial infarction due to the central role of CIRP in disease progression. Thus, future research should further elucidate the dynamic changes associated with CIRP across different repair stages, develop highly specific drugs, and conduct large-scale clinical studies to validate its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.
